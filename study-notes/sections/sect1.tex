\section{Survival Models}
\label{sect:surv-models}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The study of life contingencies\footnote{The name ``life
contingencies'' reflects the feature that we are studying things that are
\underline{contingent} on \underline{life}.} is motivated from the needs of
managing the risk arising from life insurance business \faIcon{building}.
Generally speaking, life insurance products provide benefit payments that
depend on the survival status of the life insured. For example, a life
insurance may provide \$1 million death benefit payment at the death
\faIcon{skull} of the life insured.

From STAT2902, we know that the \emph{present value} of the death benefit
payment differs for different payment times. Therefore, the timing of the death
of the life insured can greatly impact on the present value of the payment to
be made by the insurer. If the life insured dies very early, then the insurer
would need to make a rather large payment in terms of present value, hence
incurring a great loss. Thus, \emph{lifetime} of the underlying life insured is
a critical element for risk management of such products.

\item Of course, no one can tell \emph{exactly} what the lifetime of the life
insured would be, so we need to deal with it using probabilistic tools: We
shall model the lifetime using some probability distributions. This is the main
theme of \Cref{sect:surv-models}: study of survival models that are used for
describing the probabilistic behaviours of the lifetime.

\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Future Lifetime Random Variable}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Here, like STAT2902, we are measuring time in years unless otherwise
specified. When we say that a life is aged \(x\), it means that the life has
survived for \(x\) years \emph{exactly}. An important note is that the meaning
of ``aged \(x\)'' here actually differs from the daily life one \warn{}. In
daily life language, ``age'' refers to the number of birthdays
\faIcon{birthday-cake} passed, which is always an integer. But here the value
of \(x\) can be fractional, although it is often an integer as well.

For convenience, sometimes we shall denote a life aged \(x\) by \((x)\).

\item A survival model describes the probabilistic behaviours of the lifetime
via a random variable known as \emph{future lifetime random variable}. As its
name suggests, this random variable measures the future lifetime of a life
(aged \(x\)). Mathematically speaking, the \defn{future lifetime random
variable} for a life aged \(x\) is a \underline{nonnegative continuous} random
variable (denoted by \(T_x\)).

Each survival model corresponds to a probability distribution for \(T_x\).
Examples include uniform distribution, exponential distribution, etc.

\item Let us now introduce some notations that represent various kinds of
probabilities about the lifetime random variable \(T_x\):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Notation&Probability that \((x)\) ...\\
\midrule
\(\px[t]{x}=S_x(t)=\prob{T_x>t}\)&\gc{survives} \(t\) years \\
\(\qx[t]{x}=F_x(t)=\prob{T_x\le t}\)&\rc{dies} within \(t\) years\\
\(\qx[u|t]{x}=\prob{u<T_x\le u+t}\)&\gc{survives} \(u\) years and \rc{dies} in the subsequent \(t\) years
\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{note}
The ``\(t\)''s in these notations can be dropped when \(t=1\).
\end{note}

Examples:
\begin{itemize}
\item The probability that a life aged 30 survives until age 50 is \(\px[20]{30}\).
\item The probability that a life aged 40 dies before age 50 is \(\qx[10]{40}\).
\item The probability that a life aged 20 dies between age 60 and 90 is
\(\qx[40|30]{20}\).
\end{itemize}

The notations involving ``\(p\)'' and ``\(q\)'' are the so-called
\emph{actuarial notations}, which are shorthands used by actuaries to simplify
presentations\footnote{Perhaps a secondary purpose is to gatekeep and ``scare
away'' people that are unfamiliar with actuarial science? \faIcon[regular]{laugh-wink}}. It is quite normal
for beginners to find them somewhat confusing and unnecessary
\faIcon[regular]{grimace}.  Nonetheless, you would (hopefully!) appreciate them
more as you learn more about life contingencies in STAT3901 and also STAT3909.

\begin{remark}
\item \(S_x(t)\) and \(F_x(t)\) are the \emph{survival function} and the
\emph{cumulative distribution function} (cdf) of \(T_x\) respectively.  \item
In general, the letter ``\(p\)'' is used in actuarial notations related to
\gc{``survival'' \faIcon{child}}, and ``\(q\)'' is used in notations related to
\rc{``death'' \faIcon{skull}}. We use ``\(q\)'' for the third probability as it is
about \underline{dying} in a certain time interval.
\end{remark}

\item Next, let us introduce some useful formulas that facilitate our
computations of these probabilities related to lifetimes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{it:p-factorize} \emph{Factorization formula for
``\(p\)'':\footnote{\emph{(If you are interested)} This can be proven
mathematically by considering conditional probability, with the natural
assumption that \(T_0-x|T_0>x\eqd T_x\), where ``\(\eqd\)'' denotes equality in
distribution. Verbally, this assumption means that for a life alive at age
\(x\), the lifetimes obtained by the following two methods should have the same
distribution:
\begin{itemize}
\item measuring the remaining time to be lived from age \(x\)
\item measuring the time lived from age 0 (newborn) and then subtracting \(x\) years
\end{itemize}
Such assumption is helpful for relating \(T_0\) and \(T_x\), which can in turn
be utilized for relating the \(T\)'s with different subscripts. For
more details, see \textcite{dickson2019actuarial}.
}}

\subsection{Force of Mortality}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The concept of \emph{force of mortality} is analogous to \emph{force of
interest} learnt in STAT2902. Recall that for the force of interest
\(\delta_t\), we can interpret ``\(\text{amount at time \(t\)}\times
\delta_t\times \Delta t\)'' as the approximated amount of interest earned in the
time period \([t,t+\Delta t]\), when \(\Delta t\) is small.

Here, instead of considering interest, which \underline{increases} the
\underline{amount of money} \faIcon{coins}, we are considering \emph{mortality}
(hence the name ``force of mortality''), which \underline{decreases} the
\underline{survival probability} \faIcon{child}.

\item \label{it:fom} The \defn{force of mortality} at age \(x\), denoted by
\(\mu_x\), is defined by
\[
\mu_x=-\frac{S_0'(x)}{S_0(x)}=-\dv{}{x}\ln S_0(x)
\]
The value \(\mu_x\times \Delta x\) can be interpreted as the approximated
probability for \((x)\) to die in \(\Delta x\) years, when \(\Delta x\) is
small.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\draw[-Latex] (0,0) -- (10,0) node[right]{Age};
\fill[] (0,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {0};
\fill[] (3,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below left] {\(x\)};
\fill[] (3.1,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below right] {\(x+\Delta x\)};
\draw[-Latex, ForestGreen] (0,0.2) to[bend left] (3,0.2);
\node[] () at (1.5, 1) {lives for \(x\) years};
\draw[color=red,decorate,decoration={brace, amplitude=5pt}] (3, 0.2) -- (3.1,0.2)
node[midway, above=0.3cm]{\faIcon{skull}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

For example, if you are now aged \(x\) and your force of mortality at age \(x\)
is \(\mu_x\), then the approximated probability for you to die \faIcon{skull}
within the next 24 hours would be \(\mu_x\times (1/365)\) (hopefully very
small!), assuming 1 day equals \(1/365\) years.



\item After defining force of mortality, let us introduce some formulas related
to it:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{it:surv2fom} \emph{\(\text{Survival function \(\px[t]{x}\)}\to \text{force of mortality \(\mu_{x+t}\)}\):}
\[
\mu_{\blc{x}+\mgc{t}}=-\frac{S_\blc{x}'(\mgc{t})}{S_\blc{x}(\mgc{t})}=-\dv{}{\mgc{t}}\ln S_\blc{x}(\mgc{t}).
\]
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\draw[-Latex] (0,0) -- (10,0) node[right]{Age};
\fill[] (0,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {0};
\fill[] (3,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below=0.05] {\(x\)};
\fill[] (7,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below left] {\(x+t\)};
\fill[] (7.1,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below right] {\(x+t+\Delta t\)};
\draw[-Latex, ForestGreen] (3,0.2) to[bend left] (7,0.2);
\node[] () at (5, 1) {lives for \(t\) years};
\draw[color=red,decorate,decoration={brace, amplitude=5pt}] (7, 0.2) -- (7.1,0.2)
node[midway, above=0.3cm]{\faIcon{skull}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{warning}
Not differentiating with respect to \(x\) here!
\end{warning}
\item \label{it:fom2surv} \emph{\(\text{Force of mortality \(\mu_{x+t}\)}\to\text{survival function \(\px[t]{x}\)}\):}
\[
\px[t]{x}=\exp\qty(\rc{-}\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{x+s}\dd{s})=\exp\qty(\rc{-}\int_{x}^{x+t}\mu_{r}\dd{r})
\]
\begin{note}
Analogous formula for force of interest (STAT2902):
\[
\frac{A(u+t)}{A(u)}=\exp\qty(\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{u+s}\dd{s})=\exp\qty(\int_{u}^{u+t}\delta_{r}\dd{r}).
\]
\end{note}
\item \label{it:pdf-tx} \emph{Probability density function (pdf) of \(T_x\):}
\(f_x(t)=\px[t]{x}\times \mu_{x+t}\).

Verbal interpretation of this formula is easier after multiplying both sides by
a small \(\Delta t\), resulting in:
\[
\underbrace{f_x(t)\Delta t}_{\text{\(\approx\) prob.\ that \(T_x\in[t, t+\Delta t]\)}}
=\underbrace{\px[t]{x}}_{\text{survive \(t\) yrs.}}\times 
\underbrace{\mu_{x+t}\Delta t}_{\text{\(\approx\) die in the subsequent \(\Delta t\) yrs.}}.
\]
\item \label{it:fom-change-impact} \emph{Impacts on survival function after
adjusting force of mortality:}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
&Force of mortality&Survival function \\
\midrule
Addition&\(\mu_{x+s}\to\mu_{x+s}+\phi\quad\forall s\in[0,t]\)&\(\px[t]{x}\to e^{-\phi t}\px[t]{x}\) \\
Scalar multiplication&\(\mu_{x+s}\to k\mu_{x+s}\quad\forall s\in[0,t]\)&\(\px[t]{x}\to(\px[t]{x})^{k}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{note}
These formulas are helpful for handling questions involving changes in force of
mortality, which are often due to some revisions on the mortality assumption.
\end{note}
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Curtate Future Lifetime Random Variable}
\begin{enumerate}
\item For now, we are exclusively dealing with the lifetime random variable
\(T_x\), which is continuous. Now, suppose that we are only interested in the
remaining (integer) number of years to be lived for \((x)\), or in other words,
the number of birthdays remaining for \((x)\). To measure that number, we can
use the \defn{curtate future lifetime random variable} for \((x)\), denoted by
\(K_x\), which is the integer part of \(T_x\), i.e., \(\lfloor T_x\rfloor\).
Note that \(K_x\) is a nonnegative discrete random variable.

\item To compute probabilities related to \(K_x\), the typical strategy is to
relate it with \(T_x\), as we are more familiar with probability calculations
for \(T_x\). Here are some formulas: For any \(k=0,1,,\dotsc\),
\begin{itemize}
\item \label{it:pmf-kx} \emph{probability mass function (pmf) of \(K_x\):}
\(\prob{K_x=k}=\qx[k|]{x}\overset{\text{\labelcref{it:utqx-fmlas}}}{=}\px[k]{x}\qx{x+k}\)
\item \label{it:cdf-kx} \emph{cdf of \(K_x\):}
\(\prob{K_x\le k}=\qx[k+1]{x}\) \begin{warning}
Not \(\qx[k]{x}\)!
\end{warning}
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Shortcuts for Moments of Future Lifetimes}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Of course, moments of future lifetime random variable can be obtained by
definition, but usually it is not the most efficient approach. Here, we will
introduce some shortcut formulas for obtaining moments of various kinds of
future lifetime random variables:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Continuous future lifetime:} \(T_x\)
\item \emph{Capped continuous future lifetime:} \(T_x\wedge n=\min\{T_x,n\}\)
\item \emph{Curtate future lifetime:} \(K_x\)
\item \emph{Capped curtate future lifetime:} \(K_x\wedge n\)
\end{itemize}
\begin{note}
\(n\) is a positive integer.
\end{note}

\item\label{it:tx-mom} \textbf{Continuous future lifetime \(T_x\).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mean (or \defn{complete expectation of life}):} \(\displaystyle
\eringx{x}=\expv{T_x}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\px[t]{x}\dd{t}\).

\begin{pf}
Note that
\[
\expv{T_x}=\expv{\int_{0}^{T_x}1\dd{t}}
=\expv{\int_{0}^{\infty}\indicset{t<T_x}\dd{t}}
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\expv{\indicset{t<T_x}}\dd{t}
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\px[t]{x}\dd{t}.
\]
\end{pf}
\item \emph{(Not commonly used) Second moment:} \(\displaystyle
\expv{T_x^2}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}t\times \px[t]{x}\dd{t}\).

\begin{pf}
By introducing a double integral and changing order of integrations, we have
\[
\expv{T_x^2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^2f_x(t)\dd{s}
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{s}2tf_x(t)\dd{t}\dd{s}
=2\int_{0}^{\infty}t\int_{t}^{\infty}f_x(s)\dd{s}\dd{t}
=2\int_{0}^{\infty}t\times \px[t]{x}\dd{t}.
\]
\end{pf}
\end{itemize}

\item\label{it:capped-tx-mean} \textbf{Capped continuous future lifetime \(T_x\wedge n\).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mean (or \defn{temporary complete expectation of life}):}
\(\displaystyle \eringx{\endowxn}=\int_{0}^{n}\px[t]{x}\dd{t}\).

\begin{pf}
Note that
\[
\expv{T_x\wedge n}
=\expv{\int_{0}^{\infty}\indicset{t<T_x\wedge n}\dd{t}}
=\int_{0}^{n}\expv{\indicset{t<T_x}}\dd{t}
=\int_{0}^{n}\px[t]{x}\dd{t}.
\]
\end{pf}
\end{itemize}

\item \label{it:kx-mom} \textbf{Curtate future lifetime \(K_x\).} The formulas are somewhat
analogous to those in \labelcref{it:tx-mom}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mean (or \defn{curtate expectation of life}):} \(\displaystyle
e_x=\expv{K_x}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\px[k]{x}\). \begin{warning}
The sum starts at \(k=1\) rather than \(k=0\).
\end{warning}

\begin{pf}
Note that
\[
\expv{K_x}=\expv{\sum_{k=1}^{K_x}1}=\expv{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\indicset{k\le K_x}}
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\expv{\indicset{k\le T_x}}
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\px[k]{x}.
\]
(Here \(\sum_{k=1}^{K_x}1\) is regarded as \(0\) if \(K_x=0\).)
\end{pf}
\item \emph{(Not commonly used) Second moment:} \(\displaystyle
\expv{K_x^2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k-1)\px[k]{x}\).

\begin{pf}
By introducing a double \underline{sum} and changing order of
\underline{summations}, we have
\[
\expv{K_x^2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\vc{j^2}\prob{K_x=j}
=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\vc{\sum_{k=1}^{j}(2k-1)}\prob{K_x=j}
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k-1)\underbrace{\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\prob{K_x=j}}_{\prob{K_x\ge k}}
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k-1)\px[k]{x}.
\]
\begin{note}
Here, \(\sum_{k=1}^{j}(2k-1)=0\) when \(j=0\). One can prove that
\(\sum_{k=1}^{j}(2k-1)=j^{2}\) for any \(j=1,2,\dotsc\) by induction.
\end{note}
\end{pf}

\end{itemize}

\item \label{it:capped-kx-mean} \textbf{Capped curtate future lifetime
\(K_x\wedge n\).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mean (or \defn{temporary curtate expectation of life}):}
\(\displaystyle e_{\endowxn}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\px[k]{x}\).

\begin{pf}
Note that
\[
\expv{K_x\wedge n}=\expv{\sum_{k=1}^{K_x \wedge n}1}=\expv{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\indicset{k\le K_x}}
=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\expv{\indicset{k\le T_x}}
=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\px[k]{x}.
\]
\end{pf}
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Mortality Laws}
\label{subsect:mortality-laws}
\begin{enumerate}
\item So far we have primarily discussed calculations of probabilistic
quantities given a survival model. But how should we select a survival model
for modelling the lifetime? We will discuss two approaches in
\Cref{sect:surv-models}:
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item Mortality laws (to be discussed here)
\item Life tables (to be discussed in \Cref{subsect:life-tables})
\end{enumerate}
The first one is based on some parametric formulas of forces of mortality, and
the second one is a data-based approach.

\item \label{it:mort-laws-fmlas} Here we will introduce three kinds of
mortality laws:
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item Exponential (or constant force of mortality)
\item Generalized uniform (or generalized de Moivre's law)
\item Makeham's law
\end{enumerate}
The formulas for \(\mu_x\), \(\px[t]{x}\), \(\eringx{x}\) and \(\vari{T_x}\) for
these mortality laws are as follows.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
&Exponential&\makecell{Generalized uniform/de Moivre\\ (\(\alpha=1\):
uniform/de Moivre)}&\makecell{Makeham\\ (\(A=0\): Gompertz)} \\
\midrule
Parameters&\(\mu>0\)&\(\alpha>0\), \(\omega>x\)&\(A,B>0\), \(c>1\) \\
Distribution of \(T_x\)&\(\expo{\mu}\)\footnote{Here \(\mu\) is the \emph{rate}
parameter of the exponential distribution.}&\(\alpha=1\):
\(\unif[0,\omega-x]\)&(not standard) \\
\(\mu_x\)&\(\mu\)&\(\displaystyle \frac{\alpha}{\omega-x}\)&\(A+Bc^x\) \\
\(\px[t]{x}\)&\(e^{-\mu t}\)&\(\displaystyle \qty(1-\frac{t}{\omega-x})^{\alpha}\)&\(\displaystyle \exp\qty[-At-\frac{B}{\ln c}c^{x}(c^t-1)]\) \\
\(\eringx{x}\)&\(1/\mu\)&\(\displaystyle \frac{\omega-x}{\alpha+1}\)&(no closed form) \\
\(\vari{T_x}\)&\(1/\mu^2\)&\(\alpha=1\): \(\displaystyle \frac{(\omega-x)^{2}}{12}\)&(no closed form) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The formula of \(\px[t]{x}\) for Makeham's law is rather complex, but it is not
necessary to memorize it because:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{(in SOA exam FAM)} it is included in the SOA FAM-L table
\item \emph{(in STAT3901)} we can just memorize the formula of \(\mu_x\)
instead and use \labelcref{it:fom2surv} to perform calculations related to
the survival function
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Recursions for Expectations of Life}
\label{subsect:life-exp-recurs}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Recursion} is a very important concept in life contingencies. This
is the first appearance of recursion, and it will appear frequently throughout
STAT3901 (and even STAT3909!). Time diagram is an indispensable tool for
understanding the recursive formulas, and solving problems involving recursions
would become much easier \faIcon[regular]{thumbs-up} once you grasp the skills
of drawing the appropriate pictures for recursions (or just having them in your
mind \faIcon{brain}).

\item \label{it:life-exp-recurs} The recursive formulas for different types of
expectations of life are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Complete:} \(\eringx{x}=\eringx{\endowxn}+\px[n]{x}\eringx{x+n}\)
\item \emph{Curtate:} \(e_x=e_{\endowxn}+\px[n]{x}e_{x+n}\overset{(n=1)}{=}p_x(1+e_{x+1})\)
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\draw[-Latex] (0,0) -- (10,0) node[right]{Time};
\fill[] (0,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {0}
node[above] {\((x)\)};
\fill[] (3,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {\(n\)};
\draw[very thick, decorate,decoration={mirror, calligraphic brace, amplitude=5pt, raise=15pt}] (0,0) -- (3,0)
node[midway, below=0.7cm]{\(\eringx{\endowxn}\)};
\draw[very thick, decorate,decoration={calligraphic brace, amplitude=5pt, raise=15pt}] (3,0) -- (10,0)
node[midway, above=0.7cm]{\(\eringx{x+n}\)};
\draw[-Latex, color=ForestGreen] (3,0.6) to[bend right] (0,0.6);
\node[] () at (2.5,1.4) {\(\times\px[n]{x}\)};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\item \emph{Temporary complete:} \(\eringx{\endowxn}\overset{(m\le n)}{=}\eringx{x:\angl{m}}+\px[m]{x}\eringx{x+m:\angl{n-m}}\)
\item \emph{Temporary curtate:} \(e_{\endowxn}\overset{(m\le n)}{=}e_{x:\angl{m}}+\px[m]{x}e_{x+m:\angl{n-m}}
\overset{(m=1)}{=}p_x(1+e_{x+1:\angl{n-1}})\)
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\draw[-Latex] (0,0) -- (10,0) node[right]{Time};
\fill[] (0,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {0}
node[above] {\((x)\)};
\fill[] (3,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {\(m\)};
\fill[] (7,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {\(n\)};
\draw[very thick, decorate,decoration={mirror, calligraphic brace, amplitude=5pt, raise=15pt}] (0,0) -- (3,0)
node[midway, below=0.7cm]{\(\eringx{x:\angl{m}}\)};
\draw[very thick, decorate,decoration={calligraphic brace, amplitude=5pt, raise=15pt}] (3,0) -- (7,0)
node[midway, above=0.7cm]{\(\eringx{x+m:\angl{n-m}}\)};
\draw[-Latex, color=ForestGreen] (3,0.6) to[bend right] (0,0.6);
\node[] () at (2.5,1.4) {\(\times\px[m]{x}\)};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{itemize}
\item Recursions here are helpful for recalculating expectations of life after
changes in mortality assumption in early time period. For example, if there is
an adjustment on the value of \(p_x\) only, then we can utilize the recursive
formula \(e_x=p_x(1+e_{x+1})\) to recalculate \(e_x\) conveniently, since
\(e_{x+1}\) would not be affected by this adjustment, and we just need to
update the value of \(p_x\) on the RHS to get the new \(e_x\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Life Tables}
\label{subsect:life-tables}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Practically, the insurer often uses a \emph{life table} \faIcon{table} to
model the lifetime, instead of the mortality laws in
\Cref{subsect:mortality-laws}. Although there are some ``nice'' formulas
available for those mortality laws, they can only describe lifetimes in certain
``preconfigured'' ways, which limits the flexibility. On the other hand, life
table can be constructed based on historical data about mortality experience of
individuals, which allows a more accurate description of the pattern of human
mortality. Particularly, as the insurer has sold many policies, a large amount
of data is available for constructing some custom life tables.

\item A basic life table looks like the following:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
Age \(x\)&\(\lx{x}\)&\(\dx{x}\) \\
\midrule
30&10000&50.25 \\
31&\fpeval{10000-50.25}&60.11 \\
32&\fpeval{10000-50.25-60.11}&62.89 \\
33&\fpeval{10000-50.25-60.11-62.89}&70.37 \\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The idea behind the construction of life table is that we start with monitoring
the survival status of a large group of people at a certain starting age (e.g.,
30 here), and then we check the number of survivors in the group in each of the
subsequent ages. Through this, we can deduce some behaviour about the human
mortality. Fractional values appear in the life table because the values are
scaled such that \(\lx{30}=10000\) exactly (for convenience). For more
details about the construction of life table, see \textcite{bowers1997actuarial}.

\item \label{it:life-table-to-probs} Theoretically, by construction, the symbol
\(\lx{x}\) refers to the \emph{expected} number of survivors to age \(x\), and
the symbol \(\dx{x}\) refers to the \emph{expected} number of deaths
\faIcon{skull} between age \(x\) and age \(x+1\). (In practice, the values of
\(\lx{x}\)'s and \(\dx{x}\)'s in a life table are often inserted/estimated
based on some real past mortality figures.)

Practically, to use the life table for calculating probabilistic quantities
about the lifetime, we would use the following formulas (which are derived
based on the theoretical meanings of \(\lx{x}\) and \(\dx{x}\) above):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
&using \(\lx{x}\)&using \(\dx{x}(=\lx{x}-\lx{x+1})\) \\
\midrule
\(\px[t]{x}\)&\(\displaystyle \frac{\lx{x+t}}{\lx{x}}\)&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{}\\
\(\qx[t]{x}\)&\(\displaystyle 1-\frac{\lx{x+t}}{\lx{x}}\)
&\(\displaystyle \frac{\overbrace{\dx{x}+\dotsb+\dx{x+t-1}}^{\text{\(t\) terms}}}{\lx{x}}\overset{(t=1)}{=}\frac{\dx{x}}{\lx{x}}\) \\
\(\qx[u|t]{x}\)&\(\displaystyle \frac{\lx{x+u}-\lx{x+u+t}}{\lx{x}}\)
&\(\displaystyle \frac{\overbrace{\dx{x+u}+\dotsb+\dx{x+u+t-1}}^{\text{\(t\) terms}}}{\lx{x}}\overset{(t=1)}{=}\frac{\dx{x+u}}{\lx{x}}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Fractional Age Assumptions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In a life table, often the quantities (\(\lx{x},\dx{x}\) etc.) are given
only for \emph{integer} ages. In such case, it is impossible to compute probabilities
involving \emph{fractional} ages and years (e.g. \(\px[0.5]{50.2},
\qx[0.7]{60}\), etc.), without further assumptions.

To improve the flexibility, we may impose some additional assumptions on the
behaviour of the lifetime in fractional age (called \emph{fractional age
assumptions}). The fractional age assumptions to be discussed here are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{(most common)} uniform distribution of deaths (UDD);
\item constant force of mortality (CFM)
\item \emph{(least common)} Balducci assumption
\end{itemize}

\item \label{it:faa-fmlas} The following collects some key \faIcon{key}
formulas for the fractional age assumptions. While memorizing \faIcon{brain}
all the formulas below can allow you to solve problems more efficiently
\faIcon[regular]{thumbs-up}, it is not necessary to do so. Instead, one can
just memorize the most important formulas below (labelled with ``\ystar{}''),
because it is not too hard to derive the rest of the formulas just by using the
formulas with ``\ystar{}'' (try it!).

In the table below, we assume that \(x\) is an integer age and that \(s,t\ge
0\) are any values satisfying \(0\le s+t\le 1\).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
&UDD&CFM&Balducci \\
\midrule
\(\px[t]{x}\)&(use \(1-\qx[t]{x}\))&\(=\px[t]{x+s}=\underset{\text{\ystar{}}}{(\px[]{x})^{t}}\)&(use \(\displaystyle \frac{\px[]{x}}{\px[1-t]{x+t}}=\frac{\px[]{x}}{1-\qx[1-t]{x+t}}\)) \\
\(\qx[t]{x}\)&\(=\qx[s|t]{x}=\underset{\text{\ystar{}}}{t\qx[]{x}}\)&(use \(1-\px[t]{x}\))&(use \(1-\px[t]{x}\))\\
\(\qx[t]{x+s}\)&\(\displaystyle \frac{t\qx[]{x}}{1-t\qx[]{x}}\)&(use \(1-\px[t]{x+s}\))&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{} \\
\(\qx[1-t]{x+t}\)&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{}&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{}&\ystar{} \((1-t)\qx[]{x}\) \\
\(\mu_{x+t}\)&\(\displaystyle \frac{\qx[]{x}}{1-t\qx[]{x}}\)&\(=\mu_x=-\ln p_x\quad\forall t\)&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{} \\
\(f_x(t)\)&\(\qx[]{x}\quad\forall t\)&\(\mu_x e^{-\mu_x t}\)&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{} \\
\(\lx{x+t}\)&\(=(1-t)\lx{x}+t\lx{x+1}=\lx{x}-t\dx{x}\)&\(\lx{x}^{1-t}\lx{x+1}^{t}\)
&(solve \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lx{x+t}}=\frac{1-t}{\lx{x}}+\frac{t}{\lx{x+1}}\)) \\
\(\eringx{x}\)&\(e_x+1/2\)&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{}&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{} \\
\(\vari{T_x}\)&\(\vari{K_x}+1/12\)&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{}&\diagbox[dir=NE]{}{} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{note}
The ``\(1/2\)'' and ``\(1/12\)'' for UDD correspond to the mean and variance of
\(\unif[0,1]\). The idea is that under UDD, actually we can write
\(T_x=K_x+U_x\), where \(K_x\) and \(U_x\sim\unif[0,1]\) are independent.
\end{note}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Select Survival Models and Tables}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The key idea for a \defn{select survival model} can be visualized as
follows. Notably, there is a \defn{select period} of \(d\) years, after which
the effect from selection disappears.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\draw[-Latex] (0,0) -- (10,0) node[right]{Time since selection};
\fill[] (0,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {0};
\draw[-Latex] (0,1) -- (0,0.2) node[pos=-0.3]{Select at age \(x\)};
\fill[] (4,0) circle [radius=0.05]
node[below] {\(d\)};
\draw[very thick, decorate,decoration={calligraphic brace, amplitude=5pt, raise=15pt, mirror}] (0,0) -- (4,0)
node[midway, below=0.7cm, text width=3cm, font=\small]{distribution depends on current age \& age at selection}
node[midway, above=0.3cm]{``select'' part};
\draw[very thick, decorate,decoration={calligraphic brace, amplitude=5pt, raise=15pt, mirror}] (4,0) -- (10,0)
node[midway, below=0.7cm, text width=3cm, font=\small]{distribution depends on current age \st{\& age at selection}}
node[midway, above=0.3cm]{``ultimate'' part};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

A common example where ``selection'' arises is \emph{underwriting}
\faIcon{search}. The idea is that after performing underwriting on an
individual at age \(x\) (``selection'' at age \(x\)), the insurer knows more
information about the individual and thus may apply a survival model that is
different from the ``standard'' one for that individual. For example, a more
``adverse'' model may be used if it is known that the individual is quite
unhealthy.

As time passes, the information collected from the underwriting should become
more outdated and less relevant. This feature corresponds to the concept of
\emph{select period}, which specifies a time length after which the information
from underwriting is completely not applicable, so the survival model becomes
the ``standard'' one again.

\item With selection at age \(x\), previous symbols and formulas can still be
used with only one change: replacing every ``\(x\)'' by ``\([x]\)'' to indicate
the age at selection. Furthermore, we should simplify ``\([x]+t\)'' (for a life
aged \(x+t\) and selected at age \(x\)) to just ``\(x+t\)'' if \(t\ge d\), when
the select period is \(d\) years, since the effect from selection has already
been gone. Examples:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``\(\qx[u|t]{x}\)'' would become ``\(\qx[u|t]{\mgc{[x]}}\)''.
\item The factorization formula ``\(\px[t+u]{x}=\px[t]{x}\times \px[u]{x+t}\)'' would become
``\(\px[t+u]{\mgc{[x]}}=\px[t]{\mgc{[x]}}\times \px[u]{\mgc{[x]+t}}\)''.
\begin{warning}
Not ``\(\px[t+u]{\mgc{[x]}}=\px[t]{\mgc{[x]}}\times \px[u]{\rc{[x+t]}}\)''!
``\([x+t]\)'' means that the selection takes place at age \(x+t\), which is
\underline{not} the case here.
\end{warning}

Assuming \(t\ge d\), we should simplify it further to ``\(\px[t+u]{\mgc{[x]}}=\px[t]{\mgc{[x]}}\times \px[u]{\orc{x+t}}\)''

\begin{warning}
Do \underline{not} change ``\(\mgc{[x]}\)'' to ``\(x\)''!
\end{warning}
\end{itemize}

\item Probabilistic calculations for select survival models are often based on
\emph{life tables}. In this case, we will consider a more advanced form of life
table, called \emph{select life table}, which gives us some quantities with
square brackets, in addition to the ordinary ones.

A select life table can have the following two formats:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{(used in AM92 table) Format 1: Current age fixed in each row.}

For example, suppose the select period is 3 years:
