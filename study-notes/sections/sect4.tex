\section{Premiums}
\label{sect:premiums}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In \Cref{sect:life-insurance,sect:life-annuity}, we have studied the
behaviour of PVRV for life insurance and life annuity, and now we should have a
better understanding on the potential loss to the insurer, arising from the
benefit payments made in insurance/annuity products. Of course, the insurer
would \emph{not} offer the insurance/annuity products free-of-charge, and one
of the most important tasks for pricing actuaries is to price the products
appropriately:
\begin{itemize}
\item If the premiums charged (i.e., amounts of money to be paid by the
policyholder) are too low, the potential loss could not be sufficiently
covered, leading to a high risk of \emph{bankruptcy} for the insurer!  \item If
the premiums charged are too high, the insurer could lose a lot of business
\(\cancel{\text{\faIcon{users}}}\) due to market competition.
\end{itemize}
\item Pricing the products suitably is by no means an easy task. Actually, in
practice the insurer often has a whole actuarial pricing team to deal with
this, as there are many factors that influence the appropriate price to be set,
and the decision on the actual premiums charged requires a substantial amount of
actuarial judgement.

Here, we will study a somewhat simple framework for pricing the products, which
is about following some simple \emph{premium principles}, i.e., rules for
determining the premiums. While it is convenient to just follow a premium
principle for obtaining the premiums to be charged, the resulting value is
often not so realistic for various practical reasons. (To learn more, consider
having an actuarial internship in pricing team \faIcon[regular]{laugh-wink}.)
Nevertheless, this simple framework can provide us some intuition on how
different factors could influence the amount of premiums. Without further ado,
let us start discussing the premium principles.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Basic Topics in Equivalence Principle}
\label{it:equiv-prin-basic}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The first and the most frequently used premium principle to be discussed
here is the \emph{equivalence principle}. To illustrate this principle,
consider the following simple example about premiums of an insurance coverage.

Suppose the insurer offers a discrete whole life insurance coverage to an
individual aged \(x\), with death benefit (or \underline{s}um insured) \(S\).
At the beginning of each year, the policyholder would pay a fixed amount of
premium \(P\) to the insurer, whenever he is alive.

The idea of the \underline{equivalence} principle is that the amount \(P\)
should be set such that, in terms of EPV, the \gc{premiums} received is
\underline{equivalent} to the \rc{DB} paid. Observe that the premiums above
actually form a discrete whole life annuity-due: A payment of amount \(P\) is
made at the beginning of each year whenever the policyholder is alive. Here the
insurer is the recipient of the payments.

Hence, we can express this equivalence of EPVs as \(
\gc{P}\ax**{x}=\rc{S}\times \Ax{x}\), through which we can solve for
the equivalence principle premium: \(\displaystyle
\gc{P}=\rc{S}\times \frac{\Ax{x}}{\ax**{x}}\).

\item Actually, we have implicitly made a simplifying assumption in the example
above: namely that there are no \emph{expenses}. With this assumption, the only
cash flows involved are the premiums and the DB payment. However, there are
\emph{definitely} expenses in the actual practice, such as commissions to
agent, costs for processing premiums, costs for settling the DB, etc.

Thus, to reveal a fuller picture\footnote{Even with the inclusion of expenses,
it is still not the ``full picture'', as there are still many other practical
subtleties involved (to be learnt when you work in actuarial pricing team). But
here we will not consider them for simplicity.}, expenses should be
incorporated in the calculation of premiums as well. For instance, suppose that
for each receipt of premium \(P\), there is an expense of \(e=0.01P\) incurred,
due to the costs for processing premiums. Note that the expenses are to be paid
by the insurer, hence they are losses to the insurer.

The idea of the \underline{equivalence} principle remains the same. We would
like to set the amount \(P\) such that, in terms of EPV, the \gc{premiums}
received is \underline{equivalent} to the \rc{DB} paid \emph{and} the
\rc{expenses} paid. Noting the expenses above again form a discrete whole life
annuity-due, we can express this equivalence as \(\gc{P}\ax**{x}=\rc{S}\times
\Ax{x}+\rc{e}\ax**{x}=\rc{S}\times\Ax{x}+\rc{0.01P}\ax**{x}\).  From this, we
get \(\displaystyle P=S\times\frac{\Ax{x}}{0.99\ax**{x}}\).

\item With the examples above, you should (hopefully!) have a better idea about
the intuitive meaning of equivalence principle. Now, let us state formally the
definition of equivalence principle. The definition involves a random variable
called \defn{loss at issue random variable}, which has two forms, depending on
whether the expenses are considered:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{\defn{net loss}:} \(L_0^n=\pvt{0}{\text{benefits}}-\pvt{0}{\text{premiums}}\)
\item \emph{\defn{gross loss}:} \(L_0^g=\pvt{0}{\text{benefits and expenses}}-\pvt{0}{\text{premiums}}\)
\end{itemize}
Here \(\pvt{0}{\cdot}\) refers to the time-0 present value.

Then, the \defn{equivalence principle} suggests that the premium is set such
that:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{(ignoring expenses)} \(\expv{L_0^n}=0\), i.e., \(\epvt{0}{\text{premiums}}=\epvt{0}{\text{benefits}}\).
\item \emph{(considering expenses)} \(\expv{L_0^g}=0\), i.e., 
\(\epvt{0}{\text{premiums}}=\epvt{0}{\text{benefits}}+\epvt{0}{\text{expenses}}\).
\end{itemize}
Here \(\epvt{0}{\cdot}\) refers to the time-0 EPV.

\item Some terminologies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{\defn{net premium}:} premium set by \underline{equivalence
principle} with \underline{expenses ignored}
\item \emph{\defn{gross premium}:} premium with \underline{expenses considered}
(which may or may not be set by equivalence principle)
\item \emph{\defn{fully discrete}:} Both benefits and premiums are only payable
at discrete time points within policy term. More specifically, the discrete
time points refer to:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{(for premiums)} the \underline{beginning} of each year, whenever the policyholder is alive
\item \emph{(for DB)} the \underline{end} of the year of death
\item \emph{(less common) (for annuity benefits)} the beginning (for due) or
end (for immediate) of each year, whenever the policyholder is alive
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{\defn{fully continuous}:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Premiums (or less commonly, annuity benefits) are payable continuously
within policy term, whenever the policyholder is alive.
\item DB is payable at the moment of death within policy term.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Further Topics in Equivalence Principle}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The further topics in equivalence principle to be discussed here
encompass the following three themes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Theme 1: Variations on the policy.} We will introduce some variants
to the basic insurance coverages mentioned in \Cref{it:equiv-prin-basic} and
discuss how to determine the premiums via equivalence principle with such
variants: (i) \(1/m\)thly premiums and (ii) refund of premiums.

Similar to what was done before, we shall explore each variant in the following
two aspects:
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Definition:} meaning of the variant
\item \emph{Key formulas:} important formulas about the variant
\end{enumerate}
\item \emph{Theme 2: Probabilistic quantities of loss at issue random
variable.} \Cref{it:equiv-prin-basic} is primarily about calculating premiums
such that the mean loss at issue random variable equals zero. Here, apart from
its mean, we will explore more probabilistic quantities of the loss at issue
random variable: (i) variance and (ii) probabilities.
\item \emph{Theme 3: Extra risks.} Here we will explore the impacts on the
premiums with the presence of extra mortality risk, which can be incorporated
in the following three ways: (i) age rating, (ii) constant addition to
\(\mu_x\) and (iii) constant multiple of \(q_x\).
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection*{Theme 1: Variations on the policy}
\item \textbf{\(1/m\)thly premiums.} We have discussed \(1/m\)thly insurance
and \(1/m\)thly annuity in
\Cref{subsect:variations-insurance,subsect:variations-annuity}. In a similar
manner, we can define the notion of \emph{\(1/m\)thly premiums}.

\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Definition:} Premium payment is made at beginning of each \(1/m\)th
of a year in the relevant period, whenever the policyholder is alive.
\item \emph{Key formulas:} Let \(P\) be the amount of \underline{each}
\(1/m\)thly premium.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Policy type&EPV of premiums \\
\midrule
Whole life&\(\rc{m}P\times \ax**{x}[(m)]\text{\quad(often use: UDD/Woolhouse ...)}\) \\
\(n\)-year policy&\(\rc{m}P\times \ax**{\endowxn}[(m)]\text{\quad(often use: UDD/Woolhouse ...)}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{enumerate}
\item \label{it:prem-refund} \textbf{Refund of premiums.}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Definition:} The total amount of premium paid (\underline{with} or
\underline{without} interest accumulation) is added on top of the
\underline{DB}.

Thus, the premiums are ``\underline{refunded}'' when death \faIcon{skull}
occurs within the policy term. Usually this variant is applied on \(n\)-year
policies.
\item \emph{Key formulas:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Generic formula for calculating premium:} We can just insert the
added benefit (premium refund) to the equation for equivalence principle and
solve for the premium \(P\):
\[
\epvt{0}{\text{premiums}}=\epvt{0}{\text{original benefits}}
\vc{+\epvt{0}{\text{premium refund}}}+\epvt{0}{\text{expenses}}
\]
\item \emph{Formulas of \(\epvt{0}{\text{premium refund}}\) (for \(n\)-year policies):}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
&\(\epvt{0}{\text{premium refund}}\) \\
\midrule
Without interest&\(P\IA_{\termxn}\) \\
With interest&\(\underbrace{P\ax**{\endowxn}}_{\text{EPV of refund with interest}}\blc{-}\underbrace{\blc{\px[n]{x}\times P\ax**{\angl{n}}}}_{\text{no refund if not dead in \(n\) years}}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\item \emph{Calculate \(\expv{L_0}\) and \(\expv{L_0^{2}}\):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \(\displaystyle \expv{L_0}=\sum_{}^{}\text{value}\times
\text{probability}\text{ (\(=0\) if equivalence principle is used)}\)
\item \(\displaystyle \expv{L_0^{2}}=\sum_{}^{}\text{value}^{2}\times\text{probability}\)
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{Calculate \(\vari{L_0}\):}
\(\vari{L_0}=\expv{L_0^2}-(\expv{L_0})^{2}\overset{\text{(equivalence
principle)}}{=}\expv{L_0^{2}}\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
\item\label{it:l0-probs} \textbf{Probabilities for \(L_0\).} Often we are
interested in \emph{loss/profit probability:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \(\text{loss probability}=\prob{L_0>0}\)
\item \(\text{profit probability}=\prob{L_0<0}\)
\end{itemize}
There are two approaches for computing probabilities for \(L_0\). For
illustration, let's say we are interested in the profit probability
\(\prob{L_0<0}\).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Exact calculation:}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item Write \(L_0<0\iff\dotsb\iff \text{(event in terms of \(K_x\) or \(T_x\))}\).
\item Compute the probability of the event in terms of \(K_x\) or \(T_x\)
(which equals \(\prob{L_0<0}\)).
\end{enumerate}
\item \emph{Normal approximation:} If \(L_0\) is a loss at issue random
variable comprising of losses from a large number of independent policies, then
we can apply normal approximation:
\[
\frac{L_0-\overbrace{\expv{L_0}}^{\mathclap{=0\text{ if equivalence principle is used}}}}{\sqrt{\vari{L_0}}}\apxsim \ndist{0,1}.
\]
\begin{note}
If \(L_0\) is a sum of \(n\) independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
losses, then \(\vari{L_0}=n\times \text{variance of each loss}\) (not \(n^{2}\)
\warn{}).
\end{note}

From this, we have
\[
\prob{L_0<0}\approx \Phi\qty(\frac{-\expv{L_0}}{\sqrt{\vari{L_0}}})
\]
where \(\Phi\) denotes the standard normal cdf.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection*{Theme 3: Extra risks}
\item \textbf{\defn{Age rating}.} This approach incorporate the extra mortality
risk by treating the individual as a standard but \emph{older} life.

For example, an individual aged 40 with extra mortality risk may be treated as
a standard life (i.e., having no extra mortality risk), but aged 50.

\item \label{it:constant-add-fom} \textbf{Constant addition to \(\mu_x\).}
\begin{note}
We shall add a superscript \('\) to the quantities for an individual with extra
mortality risk. Quantities without the superscript \('\) denote the ones for a
normal individual with no extra mortality risk.
\end{note}

\emph{Key formulas:}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\toprule
&\(\mu_{x+t}'\) (\(0\le t\le n\))&\(\delta'\)&\(\ax*{\endowxn}'\)&\(\Ex[n]{x}'\) \\
\midrule
Constant addition to \(\mu_x\)&\(\mu_{x+t}+\phi\)&\(\delta\)&\text{\(\ax*{\endowxn}\) @ FOI \(\delta+\phi\)}&\text{\(\Ex[n]{x}\) @ FOI \(\delta+\phi\)} \\
\makecell[l]{Constant addition to \(\delta\)\\ 
(\emph{for comparison})}&\(\mu_{x+t}\)&\(\delta+\phi\)
&\text{\(\ax*{\endowxn}\) @ FOI \(\delta+\phi\)}&
\text{\(\Ex[n]{x}\) @ FOI \(\delta+\phi\)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{remark}
\item FOI: force of interest
\item The result is still applicable after changing \(\ax*{}\to \ax**{}\) (with
or without \('\)).
\end{remark}
This table suggests that the effects on the EPVs of temporary annuity and pure
endowment are the same with either constant addition to \(\mu_x\) or constant
addition to force of interest. In other words, to analyze the impact on
these two types of EPVs after adding a constant \(\phi\) to \(\mu_x\), we can
just increase the force of interest by \(\phi\) instead.

\begin{warning}
The effects may \underline{not} be the same for other kinds of EPVs. For
example, for endowment insurance EPV:
\begin{itemize}
\item constant addition to \(\mu_x\): \(\Ax*{\endowxn}'=1-\rc{\delta}(\text{\(\ax*{\endowxn}\) @ FOI \(\delta+\phi\)})\)
\item constant addition to \(\delta\): \(\Ax*{\endowxn}'=1-\rc{(\delta+\phi)}(\text{\(\ax*{\endowxn}\) @ FOI \(\delta+\phi\)})\)
\end{itemize}
\end{warning}

After obtaining the new values of EPVs based on the formulas here, the premiums
can be recalculated.

\item \textbf{Constant multiple of \(\qx{x}\).} In this approach, we scale up
the 1-year mortality probabilities: \(\qx{x+k}'=c\times \qx{x+k}\) where
\(c>1\) for each \(k=0,1,\dotsc\). For this case, there is not ``shortcut''
formula and we would need to recalculate the premiums by repeating the whole
procedure with new mortality probabilities. (This may be more practical with
the aid of a spreadsheet \faIcon{file-excel}.)

\begin{note}
Since we are scaling 1-year mortality probabilities, when we are to calculate
premiums for some \(1/m\)thly or continuous products, we would need to apply
the UDD assumption or use approximation formula for the computations.
\end{note}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Portfolio Percentile Premium Principle}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Equivalence principle is not the only premium principle available.
Another common premium principle is the \emph{portfolio percentile principle}.
As its name suggests, this premium principle is related to percentile. But what
percentile are we referring to?

\item The idea of \emph{portfolio percentile principle} is to set the premium
such that the profit probability is high. Mathematically, the \defn{portfolio
percentile principle} suggests that we should set the premium such that
\[\prob{L_0<0}=p\qqtext{or} \underbrace{\pi_{p}(L_0)}_{\mathclap{\text{\(p\)th
percentile of \(L_0\)}}}=0\]
where \(p\) is close to \(1\). Here, \(L_0\) is usually the total loss for a
\underline{portfolio} of \(n\) independent policies with identical terms,
meaning that \(L_0\) is the sum of \(n\) i.i.d.\ individual loss-at-issue random
variables \(L_{0,1},\dotsc,L_{0,n}\), and we are setting the premium to be
used for all these \(n\) policies.

\item \label{it:port-percen-approaches} The two approaches for probability
calculations in \labelcref{it:l0-probs} can also be used for calculating
portfolio percentile premium:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Exact calculation (\(n=1)\):} In general, we can perform similar
steps as the ones in \labelcref{it:l0-probs}, with probability
calculation being replaced by percentile calculation.

But there is a shortcut based on the \emph{percentile trick}.

\textbf{Condition:} \(L_0=g(T_x)\) or \(L_0=h(T_x)\), where \(g\) and \(h\) are
\underline{strictly decreasing} \emph{(more common; for insurances)} and
\underline{strictly increasing} continuous functions respectively.

\textbf{Shortcut:}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Finding \(\pi_p(L_0)\):}
\[
\pi_{p}(L_0)=
\begin{cases}
g(\pi_{\rc{1-p}}(T_x))&\text{when \(L_0=g(T_x)\)}, \\
h(\pi_{\rc{p}}(T_x))&\text{when \(L_0=h(T_x)\)}.
\end{cases}
\]
\item \emph{Calculating the portfolio percentile premium:} Solve the equation
\(\pi_{p}(L_0)=0\) for the premium.
\end{enumerate}
\item \emph{Normal approximation (for large \(n\)):}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Finding \(\pi_{p}(L_0)\):} After applying the normal approximation,
we can treat
\[
L_0=\expv{L_0}+\sqrt{\vari{L_0}}Z
=n\expv{L_{0,i}}+\sqrt{n\vari{L_{0,i}}}Z
\]
where \(Z\sim\ndist{0,1}\) and \(i\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}\). Then, using the
percentile trick, we have
\[
\boxed{\vc{\pi_{p}(}L_0\vc{)}=\expv{L_0}+\sqrt{\vari{L_0}}\vc{\pi_{p}(}Z\vc{)}
=n\expv{L_{0,i}}+\sqrt{n\vari{L_{0,i}}}\vc{\pi_{p}(}Z\vc{)}}.
\]
\begin{note}
The value \(\pi_{p}(Z)\) can be found using a standard normal table \emph{(for
STAT3901)} or the \href{https://www.prometric.com/soa}{Prometric's standard
normal calculator} \emph{(available in SOA exam FAM)}.
\end{note}
\item \emph{Calculating the portfolio percentile premium:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Method 1 (portfolio basis):} Solve the equation \(\pi_{p}(L_0)=0\) for the premium.
\item \emph{Method 2 (per-policy basis):} Solve the equation
\[\frac{1}{n}\pi_p(L_0)=\boxed{\expv{L_{0,i}}+\sqrt{\frac{\vari{L_{0,i}}}{n}}\pi_{p}(Z)=0}\]
for the premium.
\begin{warning}
Not solving \(\displaystyle \expv{L_{0,i}}+\sqrt{\vari{L_{0,i}}}\pi_{p}(Z)=0\)!
\end{warning}
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\item \label{it:port-percen-vs-equiv} \textbf{Portfolio percentile vs.\
equivalence principle premium.} So far we have discussed two premium
principles. How do they compare? There are two main features:
\begin{itemize}
\item Portfolio percentile premium usually \emph{decreases} with \(n\) and is
\emph{higher} than the equivalence principle premium. \begin{intuition}
Portfolio percentile premium principle is more ``conservative'' when there are
not too many policies in the portfolio being considered.  \end{intuition}
\item As \(n\to\infty\), the portfolio percentile premium converges to the
equivalence principle premium. \begin{intuition}
As \(n\) gets larger, the stronger ``diversification'' among the policies
reduces the gap between the two premiums.
\end{intuition}
\end{itemize}
These two features can be explained by noting that the portfolio percentile
premium is the solution to the equation \(
\expv{L_{0,i}}+\vc{\sqrt{\frac{\vari{L_{0,i}}}{n}}\pi_{p}(Z)}=0 \), while the
equivalence principle premium is the solution to the equation
\(\expv{L_{0,i}}=0\).
\end{enumerate}
