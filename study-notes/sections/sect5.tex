\section{Policy Values I}
\label{sect:policy-values}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Other than pricing insurance products, another important function
performed by actuaries is \emph{reserving} or \emph{valuation}, which is about
determining a suitable amount of money \faIcon{coins} to set aside for covering
the potential losses. This amount would depend on the chance for the benefit
payment to be triggered.

For example, consider a fully discrete whole life insurance with expenses
ignored, and suppose we would like to reserve enough money now (the start of
this year) for paying the potential DB at the year end (on average).
Intuitively, if the life insured is still quite young (e.g., aged 20) now, then
we should not need to reserve too much money, as his remaining lifetime should
be quite long \faIcon{child} and it is rather unlikely for him to die in this year.
On the other hand, if the life insured is already very old (e.g, aged 99) now,
then intuitively we should reserve quite a lot of money as there is a good
chance that the life will die \faIcon{skull} in this year.

This example suggests the characteristic that the reserve amount depends on the
future lifetime, and varies as the policy progresses (where the underlying life
insured would become older and older).

\item Like what was done in \Cref{sect:premiums}, we shall only discuss a
somewhat simple framework for determining the reserve amount, which instructs
us to set this amount as the so-called \emph{policy value}. But of course, the
process of determining suitable reserve amounts is definitely not that simple
in practice, and actually the insurer often has a whole actuarial valuation
team to deal with this task. (Again, to learn more about how reserving works in
practice, consider having an actuarial internship in valuation team
\faIcon[regular]{laugh-wink}.)
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Basic Topics in Policy Values}
\label{subsect:policy-val-basic}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Like \emph{equivalence principle} discussed in \Cref{sect:premiums},  the
concept of policy value is related to a random variable. Such random variable
is called time-\(t\) (present value of) \defn{future loss random variable},
which again has two forms, depending on whether the expenses are considered:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{time-\(t\) \defn{net future loss}:} \(L_t^n=\pvt{t}{\text{future benefits}}
-\pvt{t}{\text{future \textbf{net} premiums}}\)
\item \emph{time-\(t\) \defn{gross future loss}:} \(L_t^g=\pvt{t}{\text{future
benefits and expenses}} -\pvt{t}{\text{future \textbf{gross} premiums}}\)
\end{itemize}
Here \(\pvt{t}{\cdot}\) refers to the time-\(t\) present value, and we also
assume that the policy is still in force at time \(t\) (particularly this means
the underlying life insured is still alive at time \(t\)).\footnote{This extra
assumption is needed here, since unlike the case for equivalence principle, the
policy may have already been vanished at time \(t\) (because the life insured
has already been dead \faIcon{skull} or the policy has already been expired).
But in such case, it does not make sense to talk about future loss at such
time, which is something that does not even exist.}

\begin{warning}
There may be an ambiguity on whether the benefits/premiums/expenses
\emph{precisely at time \(t\)} should be included as ``future''
benefits/premiums/expenses. For consistency, we shall follow this convention:
\begin{itemize}
\item \underline{Benefits} and \underline{benefit}-related expenses (i.e.,
settlement expenses) \faIcon{at} time \(t\): NOT included.
\item \underline{Premiums} and \underline{premium}-related expenses (i.e.,
initial and renewal expenses) \faIcon{at} time \(t\): included.
\end{itemize}
With this convention, we can express the time-\(t\) gross loss as
\begin{align*}
L_t^g&=\pvt{t}{\text{benefits and benefit-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{>} t\)}} \\
&\quad+\pvt{t}{\text{premium-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{\ge}t\)}}
-\pvt{t}{\text{\textbf{gross} premiums \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{\ge}t\)}}.
\end{align*}
\end{warning}
\item With time-\(t\) future loss random variable, we can define the
time-\(t\) \defn{policy value}, which again has two forms, depending on whether
the expenses are considered:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{time-\(t\) \defn{net premium policy value}:}
\[\Vx[t]{}[n]=\expv{L_t^n}
=\epvt{t}{\text{future benefits}}-\epvt{t}{\text{future \textbf{net} premiums}}.\]
\item \emph{time-\(t\) \defn{gross premium policy value}:}
\[\Vx[t]{}[g]=\expv{L_t^g}
=\epvt{t}{\text{future benefits and expenses}}-\epvt{t}{\text{future \textbf{gross} premiums}}.\]
\end{itemize}
Here \(\epvt{t}{\cdot}\) refers to the time-\(t\) EPV.

\begin{remark}
\item We may drop the superscript (\(n\) or \(g\)) if it is clear from context which
kind of policy value we are referring to.
\item Intuitively, this definition suggests that we should set aside a reserve
amount that equals exactly the amount of money we would expect to lose in the
future.\footnote{This approach may be quite natural at the first glance, but
actually setting the reserve in this way is rather risky \warn{} because if the
future experience turns out to be worse than expected (not that uncommon), we
would not have enough money to cover the loss anymore, which can lead to
disastrous consequences!  This can explain why our framework here is more like
a ``toy model'' than a legitimate approach to be used directly in practice.
Practically, perhaps some extra ``buffer'' is needed.}
\end{remark}

\item The basic topics in policy values to be discussed here are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Boundary values of \(\Vx[t]{}\):} We will introduce some ``edge
cases'' in which the policy values are readily available without needing any
calculation \faIcon[regular]{thumbs-up}.
\item \emph{Shortcut formulas for WL/endowment:} In virtue of the
\emph{annuity-insurance formulas}, we can derive some shortcut formulas that
apply for whole life/endowment insurances.
\end{itemize}

\item \label{it:vt-boundary} \textbf{Boundary values of \(\Vx[t]{}\).}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Conditions&Boundary value\\
\midrule
(no condition)&\(\Vx[0]{}[n]=0\)\\
\midrule
\makecell[l]{(i) gross premium is set by equivalence principle, and \\ (ii)
\(\text{policy value basis}=\text{premium basis}\)\footnote{\defn{Basis} means
a set of assumptions. Note that policy value basis and premium basis \emph{can}
be different!}}& \(\Vx[0]{}[g]=0\)\\
\midrule
\(n\)-year term life insurance&\(\Vx[n]{}[g]=0\) \\
\midrule

\midrule
\makecell[l]{\(n\)-year deferred whole life insurance\\
(assuming premiums are payable for at most \(n\) years)
}&\(\Vx[n]{}[g]=
\begin{cases}
(S+E)\times \Ax{x+n}&\text{for fully discrete case}, \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Notations: \(S\) denotes sum insured and \(E\) denotes settlement expense.

\begin{note}
Technically, for those \(n\)-year policies, time-\(n\) policy value is
\emph{undefined} since the policy expires and is no longer in force at that
time. The notation \(\Vx[n]{}[g]\) actually means \(\lim_{t\to
n^{-}}\Vx[t]{}[g]\) (same for net-premium policy value).
\end{note}

\item \label{it:wl-endow-vt-shortcut} \textbf{Shortcut formulas for WL/endowment.}
First, we give the shortcut formulas for fully discrete whole life insurance
with sum insured \(S\) and gross premium \(G\):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Quantity&Shortcut formula \\
\midrule
\(\Vx[t]{}[n]\)&\(\displaystyle
\underset{\text{(only \(\ax**{}\))}}
{S\qty(1-\frac{\ax**{x+t}}{\ax**{x}})}
=\underset{\text{(only \(\Ax{}\))}}
{S\qty(\frac{\Ax{x+t}-\Ax{x}}{1-\Ax{x}})}
\) \\
\(\vari{L_t^{g}}\)&\(\displaystyle
\qty(S+E+\frac{G-e}{d})^{2}\underbrace{\qty(\Ax[][2]{x+t}-\Ax{x+t}^{2})}_{\mathclap{\text{Variance of WL PVRV \faIcon{at} time \(t\)}}}
\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
To obtain the shortcut formulas for other cases, perform the following changes
on the two formulas above:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{(\(n\)-year endowment)} Change \(
\begin{cases}
\Ax{x}\to\Ax{\endowxn} \\
\ax**{x}\to\ax**{\endowxn}
\end{cases}
\) and
\(
\begin{cases}
\Ax{x+t}\to\Ax{x+t:\angl{n-t}} \\
\ax**{x+t}\to\ax**{x+t:\angl{n-t}}
\end{cases}
\) (\(0\le t\le n\))
\item \emph{(\(1/m\)thly insurances and premiums)} Add superscript \((m)\) to
\(\ax**{}\) and \(A\), and change \(d\to d^{(m)}\).

\begin{note}
In this case \(G\) refers to the \underline{total} amount of gross premiums in
a year.
\end{note}
\item \emph{(fully continuous)} Change \(\ax**{}\to\ax*{}\), \(A\to\Ax*{}\),
and \(d\to\delta\).
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Further Topics in Policy Values}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In \Cref{subsect:policy-val-basic} we have studied some relatively
basic topics in policy values. Armed with our basic understanding in policy
values, we can now have some further and more advanced discussions on policy
values. The topics to be discussed can be organized into the following two
themes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Theme 1: Policy value recursions:} Since policy values are defined
at different time points, we can derive some recursive formulas for policy
values that illustrate the evolution of policy value over time. We will
study (i) basic policy value recursion, (ii) variations of basic policy value
recursion, and (iii) applications of policy value recursions.

\item \emph{Theme 2: Other types of policy values:} Apart from the net premium
and gross premium policy values in \Cref{subsect:policy-val-basic}, there are
still other kinds of policy values and we will discuss two here:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Retrospective policy values:} policy values from a
\underline{retrospective} view \faIcon{backward}.
\item \emph{Expense policy values:} policy values with expenses being the only
things considered.
\end{itemize}

\item \emph{Theme 3: Modified net premium reserves:} Reserves which are
obtained by \underline{modifying} the \underline{net premiums} in the net
premium policy value approach. A common example that will be discussed here is
the \emph{full preliminary term (FPT) reserves}.
\end{itemize}
Apart from these, there are still some more advanced topics in policy values,
but we shall leave the discussion to the section titled ``Policy Values II'' in
the STAT3909 study notes.

\subsubsection*{Theme 1: Policy value recursions}
\item \label{it:wyhiwyn-policy-val-recurs}
\textbf{Basic policy value recursion.}
To better understand recursions of policy values, we should bear in mind
\faIcon{brain} a ``slogan'': ``\underline{w}hat \underline{y}ou
\underline{h}ave \underline{i}s \underline{w}hat \underline{y}ou
\underline{n}eed (expected)'' (WYHIWYN).  Let us use the following basic policy
value recursive formula to illustrate this ``slogan'':
\[
\boxed{\underbrace{(\Vx[t]{}+G_t-e_t)(1+i)}_{\text{what you have}}=
\underbrace{\qx{x+t}(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1})+\px{x+t}(\Vx[t+1]{}[])}_{\text{what you need (expected)}}}
\]
(\(t\) is an integer).
Notations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(G_t\): gross premium at time \(t\)
\item \(e_t\): sum of initial and renewal expenses at time \(t\)
\item \(S_{t+1}\): amount of sum insured (death benefit) at time \(t+1\) (if payable)
\item \(E_{t+1}\): settlement expense at time \(t+1\) (if payable)
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}

\node[] () at (1, 1.9) {(amount reserved \faIcon{at} time \(t\))};

\item \label{it:policy-val-recurs-variations}
\textbf{Variations of basic policy value recursion.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Net amount at risk (NAAR) form:} Reorganizing the terms on the RHS
of the basic policy value recursive formula gives
\[
\boxed{\overset{\text{(what you have)}}{(\Vx[t]{}[]+G_t-e_t)(1+i)}
=\overset{\text{(what you need)}}{\Vx[t+1]{}[]+\qx{x+t}(\underbrace{S_{t+1}+E_{t+1}-\Vx[t+1]{}[]}_{\text{NAAR}})}}.
\]
\begin{note}
Since \(\qx{x+t}\) only appears in one place in this formula, it can be useful
for questions asking us to solve for \(\qx{x+t}\) given some policy values.
\end{note}

The \defn{net amount at risk}\footnote{It is also known as \defn{death strain
at risk} (DSAR) or \defn{sum at risk} (SAR).} \(\underbrace{S_{t+1}+E_{t+1}}_{\text{payment needed}}-\underbrace{\Vx[t+1]{}[]}_{\text{reserve available}}\)
can be interpreted as the \emph{additional} amount of money to be paid to dying
member over the reserve available.

This recursive formula in NAAR form expresses ``what you need'' in a different
way. Instead of dividing the situation into (i) the life dies \faIcon{skull} in
this year and (ii) the life survives this year, it partitions ``what you need''
into (i) reserve amount and (ii) extra amount over reserve.

\item \emph{Interim policy values:} (\(t\) is an integer and \(0< s< 1\))
This variation suggests the behaviour of policy value between integer time
points.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Relating \(\Vx[t]{}[]\) and \(\Vx[t+s]{}[]\):}
\[
\boxed{
\underset{\text{(what you have)}}{(\Vx[t]{}[]+G_t-e_t)(1+i)^{s}}
=\underset{\text{(what you need)}}{\qx[s]{x+t}\qty(\rc{v^{1-s}}(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1}))
+\px[s]{x+t}(\Vx[t+s]{}[])}}.
\]
The necessity of the extra discount factor \rc{\(v^{1-s}\)} can be illustrated
in the following picture:
\begin{center}

\node[] () at (7,-2) {\faIcon{skull} (prob.: \(\qx[s]{x+t}\)): \(\rc{v^{1-s}}(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1})\)};
\node[font=\small] () at (11,-4) {\faIcon{skull} Payment \faIcon{at} time \(t+1\): \(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1}\)};

\end{center}
In short, the amount \(\rc{v^{1-s}}(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1})\) is NOT referring to the
amount you need to pay \faIcon{at} time \(t+s\) (which is not year-end), but is
referring to the amount you need to \underline{save} \faIcon{at} time \(t+s\),
for the amount that \underline{will} surely be paid \faIcon{at} time \(t+1\),
if the death \faIcon{skull} occurs in the time period \((t,t+s]\).
\begin{note}
Of course, death \faIcon{skull} can still occur in the remaining period
\((t+s,1]\), but that is incorporated in the time-\(t+s\) policy value
\(\Vx[t+s]{}[]\).
\end{note}
\item \emph{Relating \(\Vx[t+s]{}[]\) and \(\Vx[t+1]{}[]\):}
\[
\boxed{
\underset{\text{(what you have)}}{\rc{\Vx[t+s]{}[]}(1+i)^{1-s}}
=\underset{\text{(what you need)}}{\qx[1-s]{x+t+s}(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1})+\px[1-s]{x+t+s}(\Vx[t+1]{}[])}
}
\]
In this case, we \underline{neither} need to add premium or subtract expenses
on the LHS \underline{nor} need to discount \(S_{t+1}+E_{t+1}\) on the RHS. The
following picture can help us to understand this better:

\end{itemize}
\item \emph{\(1/m\)thly version:} (\(t=0,1/m,2/m,\dotsc\)) The idea of policy
value recursion in the \(1/m\)thly case (\(1/m\)thly insurance and premiums) is
pretty much the same; We are just considering \(1/m\)th of a year instead of a
year at a time. The recursive formulas are analogous to the ones for annual
case:
\begin{align*}
\overset{\text{(what you have)}}{(\Vx[t]{}[]+G_t-e_t)(1+i)^{\frac{1}{m}}}
&\overset{\text{(basic)}}{=}\overset{\text{(what you need)}}{\qx[\frac{1}{m}]{x+t}\qty(S_{t+\frac{1}{m}}+E_{t+\frac{1}{m}})
+\px[\frac{1}{m}]{x+t}\qty(\Vx[t+\frac{1}{m}]{}[])} \\
&\overset{\text{(NAAR)}}{=}\underset{\text{(what you need)}}
{\Vx[t+\frac{1}{m}]{}[]
+\qx[\frac{1}{m}]{x+t}\qty(S_{t+\frac{1}{m}}+E_{t+\frac{1}{m}}-\Vx[t+\frac{1}{m}]{}[])
}.
\end{align*}
\end{itemize}
\item \label{it:policy-val-recurs-app} \textbf{Applications of policy value
recursions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Characteristics of changes in policy values over time:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \(\Vx[t]{}[]\) \underline{jumps immediately} after each premium
payment time, by the amount of that premium payment, less the corresponding
expenses.
\item \(\Vx[t]{}[]\) \underline{remains smooth} between two consecutive premium
payment time.
\end{itemize}

\(0<s<1\).

\item \emph{Refunding policy value upon death:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Settings:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Ignore expenses.
\item Fully discrete (annual) \(n\)-year term life insurance policy with
time-\(t\) DB \(S_t=F_t+\Vx[t]{}[n]\) (\(t=1,2,\dotsc,n\)).
\begin{note}
\(F_t\) is the face amount, i.e., the benefit amount before adding the net
premium policy value.
\end{note}
\item Constant annual net premium: \(P\).
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{Problem-solving approaches:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Approach 1: Recursions.} Utilize boundary policy values
\(\Vx[0]{}[n]=0\) and \(\Vx[n]{}[n]\) and apply the following recursive formula
(possibly multiple times):
\[
\boxed{(\Vx[t]{}[n]+P)(1+i)=\qx{x+t}F_{t}+\Vx[t+1]{}[n]}
\]
(\(t=0,\dotsc,n-1\)).
\item \emph{Approach 2: Using specialized formula.}
\[
\boxed{\Vx[t]{}[n]=P\sx**{\angl{t}}-\sum_{j=1}^{t}\qx{x+j-1}F_{j}(1+i)^{t-j}}
\]
(\(t=0,\dotsc,n\)).

\emph{How to use this formula:}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item Set \(t=n\) and use the known boundary value \(\Vx[n]{}[n]\) to solve for
the net premium \(P\) (assuming all other quantities are known).
\item Compute policy values at all time points of interest using the formula
with the solved premium \(P\), or by recursion.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{pf}
Applying the policy value recursive formula, for any \(t=1,2,,\dotsc,n\), we
have
\[
(\Vx[t-1]{}[n]+P)(1+i)=\qx{x+t-1}F_{t-1}+\Vx[t]{}[n],
\]
which implies
\[
\Vx[t]{}[n]=(\Vx[t-1]{}[n]+P)(1+i)-\qx{x+t-1}F_{t-1}.
\]
With this formula, we can deduce:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(\Vx[1]{}[n]=(\underbrace{\Vx[0]{}[n]}_{0}+P)(1+i)-\qx{x}F_{0}
=\vc{P(1+i)-\qx{x}F_0}=P\sx**{\angl{1}}-\qx{x}F_0\).
\item \(\Vx[2]{}[n]=(\vc{P(1+i)-\qx{x}F_0}+P)(1+i)-\qx{x+1}F_{1}
=P\sx**{\angl{2}}-(\qx{x}F_0(1+i)+\qx{x+1}F_1)\).
\item \(\vdots\)
\item \(\Vx[n]{}[n]=P\sx**{\angl{n}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\qx{x+j-1}F_{j}(1+i)^{n-j}\).
\end{itemize}
\end{pf}
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Theme 2: Other types of policy values}
\item \textbf{Retrospective policy values.} \\
\emph{Idea.} Instead of considering how much money we would expect to lose in
the future (like we did in \Cref{subsect:policy-val-basic}), we will deduce the
policy value (reserve) at time \(t\) by calculating ``what we have''
\faIcon{piggy-bank} for a block of policies at time \(t\), and then dividing it
by the expected number of survivors at that time, to obtain the ``share
\faIcon{pizza-slice} available'' per survivor.

To better understand what this means, suppose that we have \(n\) policies with
identical terms and no expenses (for simplicity). Then, at time \(t\):
\begin{itemize}
\item ``what we have'' (expected): the expected time-\(t\) accumulated
value of all the prior net cash flows received:
\[
n\times [\epvt{0}{\underbrace{\text{past net premiums (per policy)}}_{\text{received}}}
-\epvt{0}{\underbrace{\text{past benefits (per policy)}}_{\text{paid}}}]\times
(1+i)^{t}.
\]
\item expected number of survivors: \(n\times \px[t]{x}\).
\end{itemize}
Then, the ``share available'' per survivor at time \(t\) is
\begin{align*}
&\frac{n\times [\epvt{0}{\text{past net premiums}}
-\epvt{0}{\text{past benefits}}]\times (1+i)^{t}}{n\times \px[t]{x}} \\
&\quad=\frac{\epvt{0}{\text{past net premiums}}
-\epvt{0}{\text{past benefits}}}{\Ex[t]{x}}.
\end{align*}
This leads to the following definition of retrospective policy values.

\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Definition:}
\begin{itemize}
\item The time-\(t\) \defn{retrospective net premium policy value} is
\[
\frac{\epvt{\rc{0}}{\text{past \textbf{net} premiums}}-\epvt{\rc{0}}{\text{past benefits}}}{\Ex[t]{x}}.
\]
\item The time-\(t\) \defn{retrospective gross premium policy value} is
\[
\frac{\epvt{\rc{0}}{\text{past \textbf{gross} premiums}}-\epvt{\rc{0}}{\text{past benefits and expenses}}}{\Ex[t]{x}}.
\]
\end{itemize}
\begin{warning}
Again, there may be an ambiguity on whether the benefits/premiums/expenses
\emph{precisely at time \(t\)} should be included as ``past''
benefits/premiums/expenses. For consistency, we shall follow this convention:
\begin{itemize}
\item \underline{Benefits} and \underline{benefit}-related expenses (i.e.,
settlement expenses) \faIcon{at} time \(t\): included.
\item \underline{Premiums} and \underline{premium}-related expenses (i.e.,
initial and renewal expenses) \faIcon{at} time \(t\): NOT included.
\end{itemize}
This is \underline{opposite} to the convention used for the usual policy
value!

With this convention, we can express the time-\(t\) retrospective gross premium
policy value as
\begin{align*}
\Vx[t]{}[R]&=
\frac{1}{\Ex[t]{x}}
\big(\epvt{0}{\text{gross premiums \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{<}t\)}}
-\epvt{0}{\text{premium-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{<}t\)}}
\\
&\quad-\epvt{0}{\text{benefits and benefit-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{\le}t\)}}\big).
\end{align*}
\end{warning}

\begin{remark}
\item Often we just use the notation \(\Vx[t]{}[R]\) to denote both the retrospective
net premium and gross premium policy values, as further adding the superscript
\(n\) or \(g\) to this notation would make it clumsy. Usually we should be able
to tell which kind of retrospective policy value is being considered from the
context.
\item While the usual policy value is the expected \underline{present} value of
\underline{future loss}, the retrospective policy value is the expected
\underline{accumulated} value of \underline{past gain}.
\end{remark}
\item \emph{Key property:} The most important result for retrospective policy
values is the following, which tells us \emph{when} the usual policy value and
the retrospective one coincide.\footnote{These two policy values can
differ!} In such case, we have another way to compute the usual policy value,
namely via the formulas for retrospective policy value above.

It suffices to just focus on the comparison between the \emph{gross premium}
ones, since the net premium (retrospective) policy value is just a special case
of the gross premium (retrospective) policy value.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prp:retro-equal-policy-val}
Let \(\Vx[t]{}[]\) and \(\Vx[t]{}[R]\) be the time-\(t\) usual and
retrospective gross premium policy values respectively. Then,
\(\Vx[t]{}[]=\Vx[t]{}[R]\) if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The gross premiums are set by equivalence principle.
\item Bases for the premiums and both policy values are the same.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{pf}
The key \faIcon{key} is to show that \(-\Ex[t]{x}\times
\Vx[t]{}[R]+\Ex[t]{x}\times \Vx[t]{}[]=\Vx[0]{}[]=0\), by combining the terms
carefully.

Note that
\begin{align*}
\vc{\Ex[t]{x}}\times \Vx[t]{}[]&=
\epvt{\vc{0}}{\text{benefits and benefit-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{>} t\)}} \\
&\quad+\epvt{\vc{0}}{\text{premium-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{\ge}t\)}}
-\epvt{\vc{0}}{\text{gross premiums \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{\ge}t\)}}
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
-\Ex[t]{x}\times \Vx[t]{}[R]
&=-\epvt{0}{\text{gross premiums \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{<}t\)}}
+\epvt{0}{\text{premium-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{<}t\)}}
\\
&\quad+\epvt{0}{\text{benefits and benefit-related expenses \faIcon{at} time \(\rc{\le}t\)}}.
\end{align*}
Hence,
\begin{align*}
-\Ex[t]{x}\times \Vx[t]{}[R]+\Ex[t]{x}\times \Vx[t]{}[]
&=\epvt{0}{\text{benefits and benefit-related expenses}} \\
&\quad+\epvt{0}{\text{premium-related expenses}}
-\epvt{0}{\text{gross premiums}} \\
&=\Vx[0]{}[]=0,
\end{align*}
and the result follows.
\end{pf}
\end{enumerate}
\item \label{it:expense-policy-val} \textbf{Expense policy values.}

(Here we shall assume that the premiums are level, which is often the case.)
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Definition:}
\begin{itemize}
\item The \defn{expense loading} \(P^e\) is the difference between gross and
net premiums: \(G-P\). It gives us the increase in premium due to the inclusion
of \underline{expenses}.
\item The time-\(t\) \defn{expense policy value} is defined by
\[
\Vx[t]{}[e]=\epvt{t}{\text{future \underline{expenses}}}
-\epvt{t}{\text{future \underline{expense} loadings}}.
\]
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{Key formulas:} (Throughout we shall assume that the gross premiums
are determined by equivalence principle, and that premium and policy value
bases are the same, which are typically the cases.)
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{\(\text{Gross}-\text{net}\) formula for \(\Vx[t]{}[e]\):}
\[
\Vx[t]{}[e]=\boxed{\Vx[t]{}[g]-\Vx[t]{}[n]}.
\]
\item \emph{Expense-only formula for \(P^e\):}
\[
\underbrace{\epvt{0}{\text{expense loadings}}}_{\text{in terms of \(P^e\)}}
=\epvt{0}{\text{expenses}},
\]
which follows from the observation that
\(\Vx[0]{}[e]=\Vx[0]{}[g]-\Vx[0]{}[n]=0-0=0\).
\item \emph{Signs of \(\Vx[t]{}[e]\):}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{level discrete expenses over time:} \\
\(\boxed{P^e=\text{amount of each expense}}\) and \(\boxed{\Vx[t]{}[e]=0}\) (which implies that
\(\Vx[t]{}[n]=\Vx[t]{}[g]\)\footnote{ It may appear counterintuitive that the
net premium and gross premium policy values are identical in this case,
considering that the latter include expenses but the former does not. However,
this effect is compensated by the difference between gross and net premiums in
the policy value calculations.  }) for any \(t\ge 0\).
\item \emph{(more common) non-level discrete expenses over time:}\\
Here we suppose that the renewal expenses are level, and the initial expense
\(I\) is higher than the renewal expense \(e\) \emph{(typically the case)}.

Then, we have \(\boxed{e<P^e<I}\) and \(\boxed{\Vx[t]{}[e]<0}\) (which means
\(\Vx[t]{}[g]<\Vx[t]{}[n]\)) for any \(t\rc{>}0\).

\begin{note}
The negative expense policy value (in absolute value) is sometimes called the
\defn{deferred acquisition cost} (DAC).
\end{note}
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{Recursive formulas for \(\Vx[t]{}[e]\):} They can be obtained by
treating \(\Vx[t]{}[e]\) as the policy value of another ``policy'' obtained by
stripping off all ``non-expense-related'' elements:
\begin{itemize}
\item remove sum insured (i.e., set it to zero)
\item \(G\to P^e\)
\item keep initial/renewal and settlement expenses as the original amounts
\end{itemize}

Example of recursive formulas:
\[
(\Vx[t]{}[e]+P^e-e_t)(1+i)=\qx{x+t}(E_{t+1})+\px{x+t}(\Vx[t+1]{}[e]).
\]
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\subsubsection*{Theme 3: Modified net premium reserves}
\item \textbf{Motivation.} With the presence of negative expense policy value,
including expenses can \emph{reduce} the amount of reserve to be set (policy
value). This means that ignoring expenses and setting reserves as the
net premium policy values is actually too conservative.

This suggests the usage of gross premium policy value is more appropriate, but
there are also some disadvantages \faIcon[regular]{thumbs-down} of using that
(e.g., computational burden would increase). This leads to the idea of only
``modifying'' the net premium policy value approach to make it less
conservative, without switching entirely to the gross premium policy value
approach.

\item \textbf{Definition.} The \defn{modified net premium reserves} are the
\underline{net premium} policy values with the original level net premiums
\underline{modified} to non-level net premiums.

\emph{Equation of EPVs for modified net premiums:}
\begin{align*}
\epvt{0}{\text{modified net premiums}}
&=\epvt{0}{\text{level net premiums before modification}} \\
&=\epvt{0}{\text{benefits}}.
\end{align*}
This holds because the net premiums must satisfy the equivalence principle
before or after modification. A common example of modified net premium reserves
is the \emph{full preliminary term reserves}.

\item \label{it:fpt-reserves} \textbf{Full preliminary term (FPT) reserves.}
\begin{enumerate}[label={(\arabic*)}]
\item \emph{Definition:} The \defn{full preliminary term reserves}
\(\Vx[t]{}[\mathrm{FPT}]\) are the net premium policy values with the net
premiums given by:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{(beginning of first year)} \defn{first year cost of insurance}:
\[\Px[1]{x}=\epvt{0}{\text{DB \faIcon{at} time \(\le 1\)}}
=\underbrace{S}_{\mathclap{\text{DB}}}\times v\qx{x}\]
\item \emph{(the rest)} level amount \(P^{\mathrm{FPT}}\) that solves
equivalence principle:
\[\epvt{1}{\text{net premiums of \(P^{\mathrm{FPT}}\)}}
=
\underset{\text{(EPV of benefits for \blc{insurance issued to \((x+1)\)})}}
{\epvt{1}{\text{future benefits}}
=\epvt{1}{\text{benefits \faIcon{at} time \(>1\)}}}.
\]
\end{enumerate}

Here we assume the original policy is an insurance with policy term at least
\(2\) years.
\end{note}
